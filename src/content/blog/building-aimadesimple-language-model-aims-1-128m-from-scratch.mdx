## Introduction

Building a Language Model from scratch has been a core goal for AIMadeSimple from its inception. For a bootstrapped home research lab with a shoestring budget, this was always an ambitious project.

It is, thus, a significant achievement for us to be able to introduce the [AIMS-1 128M base](https://huggingface.co/AIMadeSimpleResearch/AIMS-1-128M-base) and [instruction finetune](https://huggingface.co/AIMadeSimpleResearch/AIMS-1-128M-it) Language Models.

The AIMS - 1 128M parameter model is built on a $200 budget with a custom architecture, trained completely from scratch and finetuned to have simple conversations in English.

> **Note:** We don’t claim it to be a SOTA Language Model, it doesn’t incorporate reasoning abilities. It is only able to have simple conversations and will not be able to answer factual questions or do any mathematics or reasoning.

This blog documents the journey of building AIMS - 1.

You can try the AIMS-1-128M-it model in the [AIMS-1 Small Model Playground](https://huggingface.co/spaces/AIMadeSimpleResearch/AIMS-1-128M-it-demo).

## Attempt 1 — Q4 2025: Lessons Learned

Our first attempt at building a Language Model in the final quarter of 2025 was unsuccessful. We struggled to find a clear starting point and we never quite found our footing.

Amidst the popular narratives in the AI space, claiming that building Language Models required exponential budgets and insurmountable barriers (especially for a home research lab), we decided to drop the project altogether by the end of 2025.

We took the first quarter of 2026 off, to explore other areas and determine where we could contribute the most.

This time also gave us a chance to reflect on what went wrong and, if and how we could take another stab at this goal.

Below is a summary of our reflections and our takeaways from this first attempt:

- **Lack of Clear Direction and a Roadmap**

  There is plenty of information and material available that attempts to explain how someone can build language models from scratch. These include popular resources like [Sebastian Raschka’s Building a Large Language Model from Scratch Book + accompanying resources](https://sebastianraschka.com/llms-from-scratch/) and [Andrej Karpathy’s NanoGPT](https://github.com/karpathy/nanogpt).

  Most (if not all) of these resources provide a solid foundation, but they leave off before you can reach a truly coherent language model, built and trained from scratch. Their primary advice is to use existing models by downloading their pre-trained weights and fine tuning them for a specific use case.

- **Time, Money, and Hardware Constraints**

  The traditional narrative around building a foundation model from scratch suggests it requires millions/billions of dollars, vast clusters of GPUs, and months of training time typical of frontier labs.

  This is a daunting hurdle for a bootstrapped home-lab operating on a shoestring budget, roughly five to six orders of magnitude smaller—a factor of 10⁵ to 10⁶ (100,000 to 1,000,000 times smaller)—than those of the frontier labs.

  AIMadeSimple’s resources were restricted to a maximum of $500 and a single GPU.

- **Picking a Model Architecture**

  Frontier research in Artificial Intelligence has advanced at an exponential pace over the past few years, with most mainstream progress originating from leading research labs. Notably, the definition of Large Language Models (LLMs) has evolved. In 2019, OpenAI described GPT-2 (125M parameters) as a large language model, whereas today’s LLMs scale to trillions of parameters.

  Determining the appropriate parameter count and model architecture for our constraints was a critical challenge. Most documented efforts in training small language models rely on GPT-2, OPT, or other recent architectures. Evaluating which design choices and components scale effectively down to smaller parameter regimes required careful analysis.

  A lack of clear guidance on model architectures for small-scale models was a key hurdle during our initial attempt.

- **Dataset Curation and Availability**

  Given that most foundation models are built by labs with substantial budgets and the focus of smaller labs is on fine-tuning, most popular datasets are focused on Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT).

  Datasets suitable for pre-training models in the 50M to 200M parameter range are less widely covered and require dedicated research and careful curation.

- **Lack of Defined Research Objectives**

  We failed to establish clear research objectives during our first attempt. Feeling overwhelmed by the scope of the project and the scarcity of practical guidance, we struggled to maintain focus and direction.

- **Isolation in a Field Dominated by Giants**

  Since the most prominent voices in AI belong to massive frontier labs, embarking on a project like this as a single person home lab can feel profoundly isolating.

  However, that perspective changes once you connect with the broader research community and realize others are striving toward similar goals.

### The Five Stages of Building a Language Model

Based on the above exercise, we have broken down the process of building a Language Model into 5 Stages:

1. **Model Architecture:** In this stage, you design and refine the underlying structural blueprint—such as layer configuration, attention mechanisms, and parameter dimensions—used to construct the language model.

2. **Dataset Curation:** In this stage, you select, clean, and filter the specific text sources needed to properly pretrain, fine-tune, and align the model for high-quality language output.

3. **Pretraining:** In this stage, the model is trained on datasets using gradient descent and backpropagation. The primary objective is to establish a foundational understanding of language structure before compressing world knowledge into the model's weights. This process yields a base model, which is then thoroughly evaluated to benchmark its capabilities.

4. **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT):** In this stage, the pre-trained model is further trained on targeted datasets to align with specific instructions and desired response behaviors. This step also involves evaluating the model's instruction-following performance.

5. **Alignment:** In this final stage, the model is further aligned to prefer helpful and safe outputs based on human feedback or preference optimization techniques.

Let’s look at each of these in detail.

#### Understanding Model Architecture

Understanding modern language model architectures starts with decoder-only Transformers, which form the foundation of state-of-the-art LLMs. This process involves studying the original GPT-2 architecture, exploring key advancements across models like OPT, LLaMA, Qwen, GPT OSS, Gemma, and DeepSeek, and experimenting with custom designs and hyperparameter tuning.

Extensive literature covers Transformers and decoder-only models. Some of our primary references include:

- [The original Transformer research paper](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1706.03762)

- [Sebastian Raschka’s book](https://sebastianraschka.com/llms-from-scratch/)

- [Andrej Karpathy’s video series](https://youtube.com/playlist?list=PLAqhIrjkxbuWI23v9cThsA9GvCAUhRvKZ&si=jY05EPJ-QaFf_Npq)

- [AI by Hand](https://www.byhand.ai/)

Rather than repeating detailed explanations, we highlight a few core concepts below.

Key architectural principles to keep in mind:

- Transformers were originally designed as encoder-decoder architectures tailored primarily for machine translation.

- Modern Large Language Models rely predominantly on decoder-only architectures.

- At their core, decoder-only LLMs operate as next-token predictors.

- A standard decoder-only LLM consists of the following foundational components:

  - **Tokenizer:** Converts input text into numerical representations required for mathematical operations during next-token prediction. Tokenizers operate with a fixed vocabulary, with most modern implementations relying on the Byte Pair Encoding (BPE) algorithm. Simply saying each individual token in the fixed vocabulary is mapped to a number. The input sequence can then be written as a sequence of these numbers.
  - **Token Embeddings:** Maps each token in the vocabulary to a vector of size `emb_dim`. These trainable parameters form a weight matrix typically sized as `vocab_size * emb_dim`. Simply saying each token number is converted into a vector of n dimensions where `n = emb_dim`.
  - **Positional Embeddings:** The token embeddings don’t carry any positional information about a token with respect to other tokens in an input sequence. Positional Embeddings provide positional context for tokens within an input sequence, as standard token embeddings carry no inherent spatial information. The two main types are:
    - **Absolute Positional Embeddings:** Used in earlier designs like GPT-2.
    - **Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE):** Widely used in modern state-of-the-art architectures, integrated directly into the attention mechanism within transformer blocks.
  - **Transformer Block:** The central processing engine of the model. Each block typically comprises:
    - Normalization Layer 1
    - Attention Layer
    - Normalization Layer 2
    - Feed-Forward Layer
    - Dropout Layer (present in GPT-2; generally omitted in contemporary architectures)
  - **Final Normalization Layer:** Normalizes the output from the final transformer block to stabilize training and improve overall performance.
  - **Output Layer:** Projects internal representations into the final vocabulary logits. Weight tying is frequently applied here to share weights with the token embedding matrix.

This gives us a basic understanding of the architecture of a modern decoder only language model.

When training a language model from scratch, you can choose from two main architectural paths:

- **Existing Architecture:** Replicate a proven, existing design and train it directly on your selected datasets.
- **Custom Architecture:** Design a custom setup tailored to specific requirements, categorized by the level of modification:
  - **High-Level Customization:** Adjust hyperparameters while maintaining standard architectural structural components.
  - **Mid-Level Customization:** Modify hyperparameters while also combining and adapting components from various model families to suit your use case.
  - **Low-Level Customization:** Construct custom architectures from scratch by implementing novel structural components or mechanisms.

These levels reflect where code modifications occur: hyperparameter adjustments involve configuration changes, whereas low-level customization requires editing core model source code.

With the core model architecture established, the next critical step is selecting and curating pretraining datasets.

#### Dataset Research and Selection

##### Pretraining Datasets

Dataset curation is often overlooked in mainstream AI literature, largely because early foundational models were trained indiscriminately on whatever text was readily available online. However, a growing body of research demonstrates that thoughtfully curated datasets enable small language models to be trained effectively from scratch.

Datasets play a fundamental role in shaping a language model's capabilities, as the training text directly governs its ability to accurately predict the next token. Exposing the architecture to a diverse and balanced distribution of tokens is essential for robust performance.

The token-to-parameter ratio is governed primarily by Chinchilla scaling laws, which suggest that a dataset should contain roughly 20 times more tokens than the model's total parameter count to achieve optimal learning efficiency.

Frontier research labs with massive financial backing can afford to pretrain models on web-scale datasets encompassing trillions of tokens.

However, under strict resource constraints, pretraining on vast web crawls is infeasible.

Recent studies confirm that high-quality data curation significantly elevates the output performance of smaller language models.

Consequently, for small-budget projects, data quality and curation become the primary levers for success.

Since most published datasets target large-scale LLMs, identifying or constructing datasets suitable for sub-billion parameter models requires deliberate effort.

When sourcing pretraining data, practitioners generally choose between two primary approaches (or combine them based on project goals):

1. Utilizing existing open-source datasets

2. Curating a custom synthetic or domain-specific dataset

We conducted a comprehensive review of publicly available datasets. After evaluating several options (including synthesizing a bespoke dataset), we selected the BabyLM dataset for our pretraining pipeline.

### Exploring More Resources and Research

At this point, we also reviewed additional sources, particularly research papers and playbooks:

- [SmolLM2 research paper](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2502.02737)

- [HuggingFace Playbook SmolLM 3](https://huggingface.co/spaces/HuggingFaceTB/smol-training-playbook)

- [MobileLLM research paper](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2402.14905)

Here are our major takeaways from these resources:

- The Smol Training Playbook, by HuggingFace, provides a highly comprehensive guide to training small language models. While its extensive detail can be overwhelming to read end-to-end, uploading it to a tool like Google NotebookLM makes it easy to query and extract key insights efficiently.

- **The SmolLM2 Research Paper:** This research paper focuses on Data Centric Training. It highlights the outsized importance of data curation, especially for smaller models. It highlights that data should be curated for core knowledge and fundamental capabilities. Reference research - Abdin et. al. 2024a , Rolnick et. al., 2017

- The SmolLM2 Research Paper also highlights the purpose of pretraining. Pretraining helps the model fit the structure of language (Clark, 2019) and store factual knowledge (Petroni et. al. 2019, Roberts et. al., 2020)

- The sweet spot for vocabulary size depends on the target coverage and model size. For English - only models, around 50k tokens are usually sufficient.

- GQA is a solid alternative to MHA. It preserves performance while being more efficient at inference

- When using Group Query Attention, the Query-to-KV Head Ratio should be roughly 3:1 to 4:1

- Dropout Layers are used in model architecture when the model is trained on the same dataset for multiple epochs

- Model architectures can be Dense, MoE (Mixture of Experts) or Hybrid

- **MobileLLM research:** The paper highlights the significance of model architecture for sub-billion-parameter models. Its main findings include:
  - Deep-and-thin architectures, with smaller embedding dimensions and more attention layers
  - Embedding sharing
  - Grouped-Query Attention (GQA)
  - Immediate block-wise weight sharing
  - Feed-forward layers dominate compute during pretraining, while attention becomes the main bottleneck at inference.
  - The MobileLLM baseline evaluates four design techniques for small models:
    1. Adopting SwiGLU FFN (Dauphin et al., 2017)
    2. Using deep-and-thin architectures
    3. Revisiting embedding sharing (Zhang et al., 2022)
    4. Using Grouped-Query Attention (Chowdhery et al., 2023)
  - The paper reports that models with 30 or even 42 layers perform significantly better than 12-layer models around 125M parameters. This differs from earlier 125M models, which were generally limited to 12 layers (Zhang et al., 2022; Black et al., 2022).
  - **Query and KV heads:** Balancing semantic detail per head against the number of nonlinear combinations is an important design choice. In GQA, fewer key-value heads are shared across query heads; the key-value heads are repeated when computing attention scores and outputs.
  - The paper reports that 16 query heads perform well. Reducing the KV heads from 16 to 4 produced comparable accuracy for the 125M model and a 0.2-point accuracy drop for the 350M model, while reducing model size by almost 10%.

### Revisiting and Refining Our Research Objectives

Based on our literature review, training experiments, and research, we refined our goals as follows:

1. We recognized that pretraining a language model serves two primary purposes: developing linguistic capabilities and compressing world knowledge.

2. Given our shoestring budget, achieving both objectives was infeasible. Therefore, we focused on the first: developing strong linguistic capabilities in English.

3. To quantify "strong linguistic capabilities," we drew inspiration from the BabyLM benchmark, which models language acquisition on the volume of data a human child receives. We targeted the language proficiency level of a 5th-grade student—a stage where a child possesses robust language fluency and, when paired with modern tools, can effectively perform everyday tasks.

In summary, our refined goal became:

> Build a language model on a shoestring budget that achieves the linguistic proficiency of an elementary school student and performs exceptionally well on a narrow use case.

The key architectural components and our design rationale include:

- **Custom GPT-2 Tokenizer:** Given that an optimal vocabulary size for an English-only model is around 50,000 tokens, we selected the GPT-2 tokenizer. We extended it with special tokens to support post-training tasks: `<|user|>`, `<|assistant|>`, and `<|pad|>`.
- **Token Embeddings:** We opted for an embedding dimension of 512, aligning with a deep-and-thin model architecture design.
- **Positional Embeddings:** We implemented Rotary Position Embeddings (RoPE) supporting a context length of 1,024 tokens and a base frequency (`rope_base`) of 10,000, which is well-suited for a ~50k vocabulary size.
- **Transformer Blocks:** We configured 28 transformer blocks to stay near the 125M parameter target (layer count can be further scaled if weight sharing is applied to the output layer):
  - **Normalization Layers:** Replaced standard LayerNorm with RMSNorm to improve computational efficiency with minimal impact on accuracy.
  - **Attention Layer:** Adopted Masked Grouped-Query Attention (GQA) over standard Multi-Head Attention, drawing on MobileLLM and modern LLM paradigms. We configured 16 query attention heads based on MobileLLM recommendations.
  - **Dropout:** Retained dropout regularization since training on the BabyLM dataset spans multiple epochs.
  - **Feed-Forward Layer:** Implemented SwiGLU (gated) activation with a hidden dimension 2.66x the embedding dimension (1,365 units), evaluating various expansion factors (2.68x, 3x, 4x) inspired by Qwen, LLaMA, and MobileLLM architectures.
- **Final Normalization Layer:** Applied RMSNorm prior to the output projection for efficiency.
- **Output Layer:** Used a standard linear output layer without weight tying to keep implementation complexity manageable.

We also experimented with scaling to a larger parameter count but ultimately decided against it due to two primary factors:

1. **Hardware Constraints:** The limited VRAM on a single NVIDIA G4 GPU (via Google Colab Pro+) could not accommodate a larger model architecture.

2. **Training Cost & Compute:** Per Chinchilla scaling laws, token requirements scale proportionally with parameter count (requiring ~20x tokens per parameter). Training a larger model would significantly increase both overall runtime and operational expenses.

##### BabyLM Dataset Composition

The BabyLM 2026 Strict dataset comprises the following sources:

<div class="my-6 overflow-x-auto [&_table]:min-w-[560px] [&_table]:my-0 [&_td]:break-words" role="region" aria-label="BabyLM dataset composition" tabIndex={0}>

| Source | Training File | Content | Approximate Tokens |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CHILDES | `childes.train.bin` | Transcripts from the Child Language Data Exchange System, capturing naturalistic child-directed speech and parent-child interactions | ~28.4M |
| Project Gutenberg | `gutenberg.train.bin` | Public domain literary works offering narrative depth and structured written English | ~25.6M |
| OpenSubtitles | `open_subtitles.train.bin` | Dialogue transcripts from movies and television shows, providing exposure to informal, multi-turn dialogue | ~22.8M |
| Simple Wikipedia | `simple_wiki.train.bin` | Articles from Simple English Wikipedia, offering introductory factual knowledge with simplified syntax | ~15.3M |
| BNC Spoken | `bnc_spoken.train.bin` | Excerpts from the spoken component of the British National Corpus, capturing unscripted everyday British conversations | ~7.6M |
| Switchboard | `switchboard.train.bin` | Transcripts of spontaneous telephone conversations between paired speakers | ~248K |

</div>

- **Variable Sequence Length Training**

  Our curriculum leverages variable sequence length training. Although the model supports a full context length of 1,024 tokens, we progressively scaled the sequence length from 256 to 1,024 tokens during training. Empirical support for this curriculum strategy is detailed in [this research paper](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2405.13226) and aligns with the approach used for the [BabyLM 2026 baseline model](https://github.com/babylm-org/babylm-baselines).

  The training curriculum is divided into four sequential stages:

- **Stage 1: Initial Child-Directed Focus**

  - **Datasets:** Trained exclusively on the childes dataset (`childes.train.bin` with `childes.bin` for validation).
  - **Parameters:** 2 epochs, batch size of 32, maximum sequence length of 256, stride of 256, with shuffling enabled.

- **Stage 2: Expanded Context Length**

  - **Datasets:** Continued on the childes dataset.
  - **Parameters:** 2 epochs, batch size of 32, maximum sequence length of 512, stride of 512, with shuffling enabled.

- **Stage 3: Broadened Multi-Source Training**

  - **Datasets:** Expanded to include open_subtitles, bnc_spoken, gutenberg, and simple_wiki.
  - **Parameters:** 4 epochs, batch size of 32, maximum sequence length of 512, stride of 512, with shuffling enabled.

- **Stage 4: Full Context Length Fine-Tuning**

  - **Datasets:** Trained on the combined multi-source datasets (open_subtitles, bnc_spoken, gutenberg, and simple_wiki).
  - **Parameters:** 8 epochs, batch size of 32, maximum sequence length and stride set to the model's full context length (<code class="break-all">MODEL_CONFIG["context_length"]</code>), with shuffling enabled.

#### Pretraining Run

We pre-trained the model using the four-stage curriculum described above.

We are choosing to not go into details of our other choice of hyperparameters at the moment.

###### Key Insights from Evaluations

- **Syntactic & Grammatical Strength:** AIMS-1 128M achieves a 75.10% score on BLiMP and 65.23% on BLiMP Supplement, outperforming both the BabyLM baseline GPT-2 Strict model and the Strict-Small model.

- **Overall Comparison with Standard GPT-2:** When compared against the original full-scale GPT-2 124M model, AIMS-1 128M achieves higher performance on BLiMP (73.46% vs ~66%) and EWoK (53.46% vs ~50%), highlighting the effectiveness of the updated architecture and targeted curriculum training.

- **World Knowledge & Reasoning Limits:** Tasks requiring broader factual knowledge or entity tracking remain challenging (e.g., Entity Tracking at 19.79%), consistent with our decision to prioritize linguistic fluency over factual compression on a strict training budget.

## Conclusion

This project has been an extraordinarily rewarding endeavor for our independent research lab. Undertaking the development of a language model completely from scratch on a strict $200 budget and a single GPU presented significant constraints, but it also forced us to prioritize high-efficiency architectural choices, rigorous dataset curation, and clear, targeted objectives.

We are proud to have met our core objectives: demonstrating that a sub-billion parameter model can achieve minimal English syntactic fluency and engage in natural everyday conversations without requiring immense compute resources. Looking ahead, we plan to build upon these foundation blocks by exploring 235M parameter scaling, preference alignment, and tool-calling capabilities. We hope this experiment and transparent documentation inspire other small labs, researchers, and hobbyists to undertake similar endeavors and demonstrate what is possible on a shoestring budget.
